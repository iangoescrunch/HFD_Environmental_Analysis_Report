Introduction

Human factors engineering occupies an unusual position in the built environment: it is most visible precisely where it falls short. A door too heavy to open, a ramp that demands more of the body than necessary, a sign placed where no one looks — each is a legible failure of the discipline's core promise, that systems should bend to the human, not the reverse.

This analysis focuses on the accessible ingress/egress of a historic building on the University of Central Oklahoma campus. The site presents a characteristic tension: the building predates modern ADA standards, and its retrofit accommodates code compliance without being designed, in any deep sense, for the people who use it.

Using the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX), I assessed subjective workload on both entry and exit traversals. The 13 foundational principles of Human Factors & Ergonomics serve as the evaluative framework, with particular attention to how aesthetics and emotional experience mediate functional performance — a dimension that technical compliance metrics cannot capture.

The Dirty Dozen human error precursors are implicitly operative throughout: complacency in signage placement, lack of awareness in surface gradient design, fatigue amplification in door hardware. A space that demands sustained vigilance from its users has already failed the primary mandate of human-centered design.
